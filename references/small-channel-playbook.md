# Small-Channel Playbook (roughly 0–1,000 subscribers)

Advice calibrated for big channels quietly demoralizes small ones. When the
creator is under ~1k subs (or under ~1k views/video), let this file override the
defaults elsewhere in the skill.

## The physics at this size

- **You have no browse authority yet.** YouTube has little data on who your
  audience is, so home-feed distribution is weak and erratic. This is normal,
  not a verdict on quality. Strategy: lean **search-first and evergreen** —
  videos that answer real queries can be found the day they're published and
  compound for years, building the audience data that later unlocks browse.
- **Your data is noisy.** At 50–500 views, CTR and retention swing wildly on a
  handful of viewers. Read *patterns across 5–10 videos*, not single uploads.
  Signal at this size: which topics get found in search, retention *shape*
  (where people leave), comments' content, and whether any video keeps
  collecting views weekly.
- **Silence is not rejection.** Most early videos get few views because they
  were shown to few people — the fix is upstream (demand, packaging), not
  "the algorithm hates me." Name this explicitly when creators are discouraged.

## The first ~15 videos are reps, not verdicts

Treat early uploads as deliberate practice with one skill focus each
(hook this week, thumbnail next, pacing after) — logged in the channel profile.
Creators who improve one packaging/retention skill per video outpace creators
who chase a viral topic per video. Do not let a struggling creator judge their
potential on videos 1–15; almost everyone's early work underperforms their
later baseline.

## What to do (in priority order)

1. **Pick a niche one sentence can hold**: "[who] gets [outcome] via [angle]".
   Channel drift is the #1 small-channel demand problem — every off-topic upload
   resets what the platform and viewers know about you.
2. **Choose 1–2 repeatable formats** and stop experimenting outside them until
   one shows a pulse. Formats compound skill and audience expectations.
3. **Package before you produce** (the skill's standard rule — non-negotiable
   here because a small channel cannot afford unpackageable videos).
4. **Target searchable, low-competition topics**: specific questions, "vs",
   setups, fixes, "for beginners" angles — where a small channel can rank
   because big channels ignore them.
5. **Repackage your back catalog**: any old video with steady trickle views
   deserves a new title/thumbnail — the cheapest views available.
6. **Reply to every comment.** At this size, community is hand-built; early
   commenters become the returning-viewer core.
7. **A sustainable cadence beats a heroic one.** One good video/week for a year
   beats daily uploads for six weeks and a burnout quit.

## Traps that keep small channels small

- Gear-buying instead of packaging practice (phone + window light is enough;
  see `production.md` phone tiers).
- Chasing trends outside the niche for a spike that converts nobody.
- Sub4sub, buying subscribers, engagement pods — dead subscribers actively
  hurt (they crater your CTR and watch-time signals and teach the platform
  your videos don't get watched).
- Reading analytics daily. Weekly review, monthly pattern read (the 30-day
  program builds this rhythm).
- Copying a big creator's current style — they earned attention with different
  physics; extract principles instead.
- Quitting inside the learning curve, or right after the first video that
  works ("that was a fluke") — the video after a winner is the moment to
  double down (sequel, series, cluster).

## Honest expectations (no invented numbers)

Never quote "average months to 1k subs" or success-rate statistics — they vary
wildly by niche and aren't reliably known. What can be said honestly: growth at
this stage is usually slow-then-lumpy (long flat stretches punctuated by one
video that works), the skills being built are cumulative even when views aren't,
and the process above maximizes the chance of the lumpy breakthrough without
guaranteeing it. Frame milestones on **inputs the creator controls** (videos
shipped, packaging quality, one skill per upload) rather than outputs they don't.
